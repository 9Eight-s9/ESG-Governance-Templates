This template is the practical instrument for Chapter 5 — it operationalises the supplier assessment programme described in the chapter.

🌿 Environmental Pillar (58 points)

7 criteria · Highest weighting in the scorecard. Covers GHG disclosure, Scope 3 engagement, science-based targets, renewable energy, water management, waste/circular economy, and chemical management.

  • GHG disclosure (weight 15): the most heavily weighted criterion because it underpins Scope 3 Cat 1 primary data collection
  • SBTi target (weight 10): signal of long-term decarbonisation commitment
  • Scope 3 engagement (weight 10): whether the supplier is itself managing its own supply chain emissions
  • ESRS E1, E2, E3, E5 and GHG Protocol coverage across all criteria

👥 Social Pillar (46 points)

6 criteria · ILO core labour standards, living wage, OHS, DEI, HRDD, modern slavery.

  • ILO compliance (weight 12): highest social weight — legally enforceable through EU CSDDD and UK Modern Slavery Act
  • HRDD (weight 8): EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CS3D) makes this mandatory for in-scope companies from 2026
  • Living wage (weight 8): increasingly a Tier 1 ESG procurement minimum, not aspirational
  • H&S performance via LTIR/TRIR (weight 8): BRSR Core mandatory KPI; ISO 45001 certification as gold standard

🏛 Governance Pillar (16 points)

4 criteria · Board oversight, anti-corruption, data privacy, reporting quality.

  • ESG governance structure (weight 5): board-level ESG committee + ESG-linked pay = gold standard
  • Anti-bribery & anti-corruption (weight 5): UK Bribery Act adequate procedures defence requires supply chain compliance
  • Data privacy (weight 3): GDPR supply chain obligations; increasingly material for tech suppliers
  • ESG reporting quality (weight 3): CDP responder + assured GRI report = 5/5

🤝 Engagement Pillar (4 points)

2 criteria · Responsiveness and collaboration commitment.

  • Responsiveness (weight 2): non-responsive suppliers auto-score 1/5 — a key red flag for ESG procurement
  • Collaboration (weight 2): joint emissions reduction roadmap is the gold standard for Tier 1 strategic suppliers
  • Low weight by design — engagement quality should not override environmental and social performance in scoring

🎯 Scoring and Tier System

Weighted scoring across 19 criteria produces a 0–100% overall score.

  • Leading (≥80%): verified ESG leader — maintain relationship, share as peer best practice
  • Progressing (60–79%): good baseline — set improvement targets, annual review
  • Developing (40–59%): gaps identified — engagement plan required, 6-month check-in
  • At Risk (<40%): critical gaps — immediate engagement, escalation to CPO, contractual remediation notice

Tier 1 (Strategic) suppliers should be scored annually; Tier 2 every 2 years; Tier 3 every 3 years or on risk trigger.

⚠️ Regulatory Drivers

Five regulatory instruments are driving supplier ESG assessment from voluntary to mandatory:

  • EU CSDDD (CS3D): mandatory HRDD for companies >1,000 employees / >€450M turnover from 2027; extends to Tier 1+ suppliers
  • CSRD ESRS E1: Scope 3 Cat 1 GHG disclosure requires primary supplier data
  • UK Modern Slavery Act: supply chain audit obligation for companies >£36M turnover
  • BRSR Core: % of value chain partners assessed on ESG parameters — mandatory disclosure
  • SBTi FLAG/Manufacturing: supplier engagement covering >67% of Scope 3 emissions

⚠ The Most Common Supply Chain ESG Failures:
(1) Assessing only Tier 1 suppliers while material labour and environmental risks sit in Tier 2 and 3 — Rana Plaza (2013) and similar disasters involved sub-contractors invisible to buyer ESG programmes.
(2) Sending questionnaires without acting on results — creates questionnaire fatigue among suppliers and reputational risk when non-compliance is documented but not remediated.
(3) Not incorporating ESG standards contractually — a published Supplier Code of Conduct with no contract reference is legally unenforceable.
(4) Using spend as a proxy for ESG exposure — a small specialist supplier in a high-risk geography or sector may represent more ESG exposure than a large domestic supplier with 10x the spend.
